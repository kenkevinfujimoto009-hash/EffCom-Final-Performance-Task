About the advocacy · Roll call

Poetry and art are how a people remembers itself.

The Living Poets Society asks one question: are Filipinos still making room for poetry, art and the languages they are written in?

The issue

Adult non-school book reading fell from 54% in 2012 to 42% in 2023 (Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2024a). About 40 of the country's roughly 135 languages are endangered (Campued, 2025). In 2022, Filipino 15-year-olds ranked sixth-lowest of 81 countries in reading (Chi, 2023). Poems and art live in readers and in languages, and both are under strain.

Why it matters

Poetry and art carry a people's language, memory and humor, and they help young people put feelings into words. Reviews of the research link arts activities to better health and well-being (Fancourt & Finn, 2019; The Wallace Foundation, 2025). When poems and art classes fade, a free and accessible form of expression fades with them.

Who is affected

Students who rarely meet poetry or art taught by specialists, Filipino and literature teachers, speakers of endangered languages, and the local poets, poetic debate performers and artists who need audiences to keep their craft alive.

The Society · Inspired by Dead Poets Society

A film that made poetry feel alive.

Dead Poets Society (1989, directed by Peter Weir) is set at a strict boys' boarding school in 1959. A new English teacher, John Keating, asks his students to treat poetry as something to live with, not just memorize. The boys revive a secret club that meets in a cave to read verse aloud, and they start to see their own lives differently (Weir, 1989).

The Philippines has a stage of its own for verse: the poetic debate, a contest of arguing in verse that was first held in 1924 (Manipon, 2024). This society exists to keep that stage lit.

Carpe diem.

Latin for “seize the day.” The phrase comes from the Roman poet Horace's Odes, and the film's teacher hands it to his students as a challenge.

Read it aloud.

Poetry began as a spoken art, and the Philippines still keeps that habit alive in poetry recitation, poetic debate and spoken word contests (The Manila Times, 2024). Read a poem to a friend.

See from a new angle.

In the film, a teacher stands on his desk to show that a familiar room looks different from somewhere new. Try it: write about your neighborhood from a spot where you have never stood, or in the language you speak at home.

Contribute a verse.

Walt Whitman's line about contributing a verse gives the film its big question: what will yours be? A four-line poem, a sketch or a mural all count (Whitman, 1892).

Four pillars

The school in the film boasts four pillars. This society has four of its own.

Language

Speak, read and write in the languages you carry, at home and at school.

Heritage

Collect the songs, riddles and stories your elders know before they are lost.

Art

Make something with your hands every week, even if nobody grades it.

Voice

Say your poem out loud, and say what you think about arts time in your school.

Rip out the chart

In one famous class scene, the teacher has his students tear out a textbook introduction that rates poems on a graph (Weir, 1989). The charts on this site count readers, speakers and classes. They never rank what a poem is worth.

When the Verses Go Quiet: Poetry and Art in the Philippines

A look at what national surveys, language records and school reports say about reading, heritage and arts learning in the Philippines, what they cannot prove, and what young Filipinos can do.

Living Poets Society · Updated November 11, 2026

Many Filipino children first meet verse in a lullaby, a riddle or a line on a teacher's board. Yet recent surveys and language records suggest that fewer Filipinos are reading for pleasure, that dozens of our languages are in danger, and that the classes where students meet literature and art are changing. This article walks through what the evidence shows, what it cannot prove, and where the fire still burns.

Fewer Filipinos are reading for pleasure

The National Book Development Board (NBDB) tracks readership through national surveys. In its 2023 survey, run by Social Weather Stations (SWS) with about 2,400 adults and 2,400 children, 42% of adults and 47% of children aged 8 to 17 said they read non-school books (Ombay, 2024). For adults, earlier surveys found 61% in 2007 and 54% in 2012 (Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2024a).

Setting the 2017 outlier aside, the trend is a steady decline of about 12 points between 2012 and 2023 (Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2024a). The 2017 figure for children, 93%, was also measured differently from the 47% of 2023, so it is not a fair comparison either (Ombay, 2024).

Why are fewer people reading? Among both adults and children, the most common answer, at 38%, was that other leisure activities seemed more interesting. People also cited little time, unfamiliar words and a lack of interesting topics. Limited awareness of public libraries, and the travel time to reach them, were barriers as well. Three in four still prefer printed books, 74% of adults and 75% of children (Ombay, 2024).

Reading skill is a worry too

In the 2022 PISA assessment, Filipino 15-year-olds scored 347 in reading, up from 340 in 2018, yet sixth-lowest among the 81 participating countries (Chi, 2023). The seven-point gain is small progress. Reading a poem asks for the same basic skills as any reading, such as following meaning and noticing words, so weak reading makes poems harder to enjoy.

Languages carry the verses

The Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino (KWF) counts about 135 languages in the Philippines and classes 40 of them as endangered: 22 in Luzon, 10 in the Visayas and 8 in Mindanao. Árta, spoken in Nagtipunan, Quirino, is the most at risk, with only about 10 speakers left according to KWF's 2021 validation (Campued, 2025). Songs, chants, riddles and stories live inside languages, so when a language fades, the verses it carries can fade with it.

Filipino and Philippine literature left the college core

In 2013, a CHED memorandum removed Filipino language and Philippine literature from the required general education courses in colleges and universities. CHED's position was that these subjects are already taught in basic education under K to 12. Professors' and students' groups, including the alliance Tanggol Wika, fought the change. The Supreme Court upheld the order on October 9, 2018, overturning a restraining order issued in 2015 (Mongaya, 2018). The author of that report teaches in a department that campaigned against the change, so read it as one side's account. The court decision is the fact to rely on.

Arts classes: more questions than numbers

Students usually meet art and music in MAPEH, the subject that bundles Music, Arts, Physical Education and Health. A Panay News report from October 2025 said, citing other reports, that over 62% of public high school teachers nationwide teach subjects outside their major, with examples including English teachers handling MAPEH. A division superintendent in Iloilo told the paper such mismatches are rare and temporary in his province (Galigao, 2025). In September 2024, DepEd also gave schools more freedom to set class schedules, including an option where MAPEH meets four times a week instead of five (Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2024b). Neither report measures how much students learn in art or music, so treat these as signs of strain, not proof of decline.

Where the fire still burns

Passion for verse has not died. The National Commission for Culture and the Arts (NCCA) ran Spirit: Cultural Symbol of the Philippines, a contest for students from all levels, with categories for poetry recitation, poetic debate, traditional love song and spoken word poetry (The Manila Times, 2024). In April 2024, ten groups competed in the Cultural Center of the Philippines’ poetic debate-and-rap contest, marking 100 years since the first poetic debate on April 6, 1924. Bagong Koronadal Advocates from South Cotabato won (Manipon, 2024). What fades without audiences, teachers and time is the everyday habit, not the talent.

Why it matters

A large World Health Organization review found evidence that the arts support health and well-being across the lifespan (Fancourt & Finn, 2019). A 2025 RAND review of 177 studies, summarized by The Wallace Foundation, found that arts engagement supports youth well-being through self-expression, a sense of agency, social connection and skill building (The Wallace Foundation, 2025). Those reviews draw on many countries. We did not find a Philippine study measuring the same effects, which is a gap a school survey could start to fill.

Reflect before you scroll on

  • What poem, song or riddle can you still recite?
  • When did you last write or draw because you wanted to, not because it was a requirement?
  • Who in your neighborhood or school keeps poetry or art alive, and have they ever had a full audience?
